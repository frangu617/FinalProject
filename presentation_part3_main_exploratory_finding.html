Speaker notes · About 30 seconds

Our main exploratory finding is the imbalance between hand categories. Across the combined supplied files, high-card hands and one-pair hands account for about ninety-two percent of records. All eight other categories share the remaining eight percent. The chart shows how often categories occur in this dataset. These frequencies describe the examples available to us; they are not model performance results.

A little professor tip

With just one validation royal flush, its recall can be 0% or 100%. Always explain how many examples support a rare-category score.

Speaker notes · About 30 seconds

That imbalance makes overall accuracy easy to misread. Always guessing high card would be correct about half the time in the combined files, even while missing every other category. There are only eight royal-flush records across those files. After preparation, only one royal flush appears in validation. Its result cannot establish reliability. We therefore need category-level scores alongside accuracy. Next, we will explain how we compare the models.

Sources and presentation notes

Dataset: UCI Poker Hand, Cattral, R. & Oppacher, F. (2002), DOI: 10.24432/C5KW38. Counts were recomputed from the supplied poker+hand.zip and agree with the exploratory reading draft. The chart groups the ten recorded categories into three groups and uses a linear percentage scale.

The combined-file counts include the original records in both files. The 50.12% reference is a calculation from those frequencies, separate from held-out baseline performance. The training and validation split is documented in the training notebook: five supplied royal-flush training rows become four distinct development hands, with three assigned to training and one to validation.

Earlier descriptive exploration included the test file. Model selection uses validation results. The notebook reports scores for all ten categories, and rare-category support must accompany interpretation.
